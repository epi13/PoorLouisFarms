A tidy calendar suggests that spring is for sowing, summer is for harvest, autumn is for processing, and winter is for rest. Farm work in Alaska is less tidy and more observant.

Long summer light can drive quick growth. A cold start, wet stretch, smoke, wind, or an early frost can shift the useful window. Different plants answer those conditions differently, and wild plants do not follow a production plan simply because a maker is ready.

## Plan in winter, revise in spring

Winter is where notes become next year's questions: Which seed was dependable? What dried well? Which batch needed more time? What tool caused friction? Planning matters, but it is a draft.

Spring supplies the edits. Seedlings, snowmelt, soil, and new growth tell the farm what is actually possible. Young spruce tips, for example, are useful for only a short stage. Calendula follows a cultivated garden rhythm. Fireweed leaves change as the plant matures.

## Summer contains several seasons

“Summer harvest” can mean many separate windows. One week may be about leaves; another about flowers, fruit, or garden maintenance. Sorting, cleaning, drying, infusing, and fermenting begin while other plants still need care.

This overlap is one reason small batches make sense. A manageable quantity can move through the right process when the ingredient is ready, without pretending every product should be made at once.

## Autumn is both finish and record

Later fruit and cooler nights turn attention toward finishing, blending, packaging, and comparison. A useful field note records more than success: weather, timing, texture, aroma, and what should change.

That record prevents the seasonal story from becoming a myth of perfect repetition. Poor Louis Farms emphasizes experimentation precisely because each year provides a slightly different set of materials and constraints.
