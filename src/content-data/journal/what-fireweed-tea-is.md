Fireweed tea is an herbal infusion made from the leaves of fireweed. It is not a tea made from the bright magenta flowers, and it does not come from the tea plant, *Camellia sinensis*. That makes it naturally caffeine-free unless a maker blends it with a caffeinated ingredient.

At Poor Louis Farms, the distinction is simple and important: the current loose-leaf tea contains 100% fireweed leaves. The Fireweed Pu'er also begins with fireweed leaves, then follows a fermentation and aging path that creates a different cup.

## The plant behind the name

The accepted scientific name used in this guide is *Chamerion angustifolium*, in the evening primrose family. Older and regional references may use *Epilobium angustifolium*. The plant is common across Alaska and other northern regions, especially in open or recently disturbed ground. It is famous for appearing after fire, but burns are only one of many places it grows.

The flowers make fireweed easy to recognize from a distance. The useful lesson for tea drinkers, though, is to look farther down the stalk. Long, narrow leaves carry the material that becomes the infusion.

## One plant, two expressions

Poor Louis Farms describes its straightforward loose-leaf fireweed tea as mild, smooth, slightly sweet, and earthy. Drying makes the leaves stable enough to store and brew while preserving their herbal character.

The Fireweed Pu'er is described as deeper, earthier, and maltier. “Pu'er” here communicates an aged, fermented style rather than claiming that the Alaska-grown plant is Chinese tea or that it comes from Yunnan. The ingredient remains fireweed leaf.

Neither style needs a wellness promise to be interesting. The plant, the handling, and the difference between a fresh-tasting dried leaf and a fermented one are already plenty to explore.

## A note for gatherers

Knowing a product's ingredient is not the same as being ready to gather it. Positive identification, land permission, clean sites, and careful harvesting all matter. Use a reliable Alaska reference or learn with an experienced local person. Never rely on a single photograph or plant-identification app.
